## Article antipatterns

Use these as editorial smell tests, not absolute bans. One instance may be fine. Repetition usually signals weak prose.

### Throat-clearing opener

An opening that announces the topic instead of making a point. If you can delete the paragraph and lose nothing, cut it.

Example: "In today's fast-changing world, conversations about leadership matter more than ever."

### Overused intensifier

Modifiers that try to manufacture force instead of earning it with specifics.

Example: "This incredibly important and deeply transformative shift is reshaping the entire landscape."

### Staccato burst

Too many short sentences in a row. The rhythm becomes choppy and performative.

Example: "This matters. Teams know it. Leaders feel it. Budgets show it. Nothing is simple."

### Em dash overuse

Using em dashes as the default punctuation for every break, aside, or emphasis. The dash becomes a crutch that flattens sentence variety and makes prose feel breathless. Good for occasional dramatic pauses; bad as a comma/semicolon/colon substitute across an entire piece.

Example: "The system — which I built myself — was fast — but not reliable — especially under load — which was the whole problem."

Suggestion: max 2 em dashes per article. Replace the rest with commas, colons, semicolons, or parentheses depending on the relationship.

### Elevated register

Choosing more formal or ornate words when simple ones would be clearer. It performs intelligence rather than demonstrating it.

Example: "Organizations must utilize new frameworks to commence meaningful transformation."

### One-point dilution

Restating the same claim in slightly different words without adding evidence, precision, or nuance.

Example: "The rollout was confusing. It created confusion across the team. People were left unclear about what changed."

### Repeated restatement

The same point returning later in the piece, re-explained in different words as if it were new. Unlike one-point dilution's run of consecutive sentences, this spreads the repetition across paragraphs or sections, so it reads like thoroughness while adding nothing.

Example: "The migration was rushed. ... Later: As noted, speed was prioritized over care. ... Later still: The real issue was the pace of the migration."

Suggestion: keep the strongest instance, delete the rest.

### Metaphor crutch

Leaning on metaphor when direct description would be clearer. The image stands in for analysis.

Example: "The new policy is a compass in the storm of modern work."

### Grandiose stakes

Inflating the importance of a point without showing why the stakes are actually large.

Example: "How your team writes meeting notes could determine the future of your company."

### Triple construction

Using three-part phrasing for borrowed rhetorical weight rather than real distinction.

Example: "The platform is faster, smarter, cleaner."

### False contrast formula

Using the `it's not X, it's Y` structure as a shortcut to sound sharp or contrarian. It often flattens nuance and replaces argument with posture.

Example: "Success isn't about talent. It's about mindset."

### Parallel sentence construction

Mirroring a sentence's structure in the next one to create a beat ("X did A. Y did not."). The symmetry supplies the punch, so the two halves get read as a complete thought even when the second says little or the comparison doesn't hold.

Example: "Discovery got several times cheaper. Triage did not."

Suggestion: say what the second half actually means. "Discovery got much cheaper, while triage cost the same and became the bottleneck."

### Sycophantic framing

Flattering the reader, audience, or subject to buy goodwill before making a point.

Example: "Smart leaders like you already understand that nuance is everything here."

### Balanced-take hedging

Adding so much caution and symmetry that the piece never commits to a real claim.

Example: "There are reasonable arguments on both sides, and the truth probably lies somewhere in the middle."

### Unnecessary elaboration

Explaining past the point of clarity. The sentence keeps going after the meaning is already clear.

Example: "The form asks for an email address, meaning the address you use for email communication online."

### Empathy performance

Signaling sensitivity or moral awareness without adding substance, clarity, or action.

Example: "It's important to acknowledge that this issue may feel difficult and complex for many people."

### Pivot paragraph

A paragraph whose main job is to announce a transition instead of advancing the argument.

Example: "With that context in mind, it is now worth turning to what this means for the broader conversation."

### Historical analogy

Borrowing significance from a famous past event without showing a real structural comparison.

Example: "This AI moment is our generation's printing press."

### False vulnerability

A staged confession or admission used to signal honesty rather than deepen the argument.

Example: "I'll be honest: I used to think this was simple. I was wrong."

### Abstract noun pileup

Stacking conceptual nouns instead of naming actors, actions, or consequences. It sounds weighty while staying vague.

Example: "Real progress requires alignment, ownership, capability, and transformation."

### Buzzword stack

Packing multiple fashionable terms into one sentence to simulate sophistication. Usually a sign the claim is still unfocused.

Example: "Teams need a holistic, agile, human-centered, future-ready operating model."

### Citation mirage

Referring to "research," "studies," or "data" without naming the source or showing the result. It borrows authority without supplying evidence.

Example: "Research shows that trust is now the key driver of performance."

### Quote drop

Dropping in a famous quote as a substitute for explanation. If the point survives after deleting the quote, the quote was probably ornamental.

Example: "'What gets measured gets managed,' as the saying goes."

### Borrowed cliché

Reaching for a familiar idiom to add force when its original context doesn't apply: the borrowed drama stands in for the actual point. "Survives contact with X" is the common offender (via the military aphorism); so are sports and startup stock phrases.

Example: "Most survey designs don't survive contact with real data."

Suggestion: state what actually happens. "Most survey items fall apart once real respondents answer them."

### Prescriptive voice

Telling the reader what to do instead of inviting them to consider it. Imperatives ("do X", "never Y", "always Z") make advice read as policy, and they claim more certainty than most evidence carries.

Example: "Instrument the five dimensions, report at team level, and never rank individuals."

Suggestion: soften to an invitation ("consider…", "worth trying…", "one option…") or describe the behavior without commanding it ("teams that report at team level tend to…"). One direct imperative per piece is usually fine.

### Authoritative assertion

Stating a conclusion as flat fact ("it is X", "X is the bottleneck") with no attribution. The oracle tone claims more certainty than the evidence supports, and it invites argument with the author instead of the data.

Example: "The bottleneck is review, not generation."

Suggestion: attribute to a named actor: "In Faros's telemetry, review was the bottleneck, not generation." Name the study, dataset, or guide rather than a vague "research shows" (see citation mirage). One attribution per claim is enough; stacking hedges is its own antipattern (see balanced-take hedging).
